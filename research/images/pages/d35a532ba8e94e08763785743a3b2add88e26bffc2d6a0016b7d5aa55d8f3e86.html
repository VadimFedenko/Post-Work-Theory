Relativistic landings are generally not recommended for complex systems. So some sort of deceleration and landing would be a good idea. Gamma rays are also dangerous radiation, and not recommended if lifeforms are anywhere nearby; but they have the advantage of capable of tight collimation.

In most other ways this is basically the idea behind the Lightways, and particularly the various engenerator colonies established in many parts of the Terragen Sphere.

RE: Amalgam and Aloof - Drashner1 - 08-13-2025

(08-13-2025, 05:11 AM)Tom Mazanec Wrote: Particularly interesting is exploring a new system by firing a "spore" at relativistic velocity which lands on a planet of the system, and proceeds to grow a gamma-ray receiver for the explorers to beam themselves to.

Note that in the Amalgam story I read where this is done, the 'spore' is an antimatter rocket that accelerates so fast that time dilation stretches out its existence to external observers long enough to reach the star. It actually doesn't land on the planet but instead impacts the star very precisely such that a series of shockwaves propagate through the star, forming components for nanobots which are expelled from the opposite side of the star such that they both come together to self-assemble into nanobots in space and then eventually get captured by the gravity of a local gas giant and then one of its moons - which is what they land on and then create a gamma ray laser receiver and hardware to grow bodies and such. Which is an example of precision shooting if ever there was one.

The Amalgam civilization is millions of years old, so presumably has had time to work out how to do this kind of thing. OA tech is not up to that level of making things happen at a distance but has seedships that can transport spores to build Lightways receivers and such. There's even something called a Starspark (currently just living in my head) that we've discussed a bit. It would be a mesh of magmatter and slow down by 'aerobraking' at the destination star, before going on into a local system to do whatever its creator wanted done. It would be S3 tech.

I should get that written up at some point.

RE: Amalgam and Aloof - Rynn - 08-14-2025

The starspark is the more plausible version of the idea, using exotic matter that can survive "lithobreaking" into a star. The spore method in Amalgam/Aloof is more like smashing a rock into an ocean in such a way that the turbulence smashes organic molecules into fully formed, single celled organisms (an ocean whose state at time of impact you can't know when you throw the rock). Not that that really matters though, it's a really fun idea regardless.

I like the Amalgam/Aloof universe because, for the most part, it's like a hard-SF version of the Culture. The galaxy isn't a single civilisation due to interstellar distances, but virtually anywhere you go you can be treated with respect, enjoy post scarcity living, live in harmony among others etc. I find the short stories set in A/A more enjoyable than the one novel. Like a lot of Egan's work the novel is more of an exercise in exploring one hard-SF idea in a lot of detail, in this case "could a pre-industrial species figure out general relativity." I can't say the premise captivated me long enough for an entire novel. Though I did enjoy that characters who were explorers of the galactic core at one point stopped and let their self-replicating machines grow a hypertelescope array to map out where to go next.

My favourite story in the setting is probably Riding the Crocodile. It's a poignant story showing the kinds of things people might dedicate themselves to even with eternal life and wealth.
