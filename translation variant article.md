Imagine the near future: AI can replace intellectual labor, while robots can replace physical labor. Let’s leave the questions of timelines and feasibility aside, and just accept the premise: machines are no worse than humans in any way and can perform any task.

What happens next?

Nobody knows anyway, but we can take a walk through the space of possibilities and look at what kinds of societies could emerge in principle.

The very first objection: just because machines *can* do our work doesn’t mean we will actually give it to them! And it certainly doesn’t mean people will stop working. So far, humanity has kept on working despite growing automation.

*Dune* solved this in the most radical way. After a machine uprising and the [Butlerian Jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butlerian_Jihad), thinking machines were completely banned. Calculations are done by human computers, while Reverend Mothers serve as living databases. In other words, people avoided AI automation in a rather amusing way: instead of machines replacing humans, humans themselves replaced basic computers.

Could we end up in the world of *Dune*? We do have the options: neo-Luddites calling to bomb data centers, the Unabomber’s manifesto, and various forms of anarcho-primitivism.

There is also what is essentially an inverted *Dune*: in *Horizon Zero Dawn*, there was also a war with machines, but humans lost. Ironically, the need to work returned here too. Humans ended up in an archaic world filled with robot dinosaurs, where they have to survive by hunting and gathering.

And in *Her*, by the end of the movie, the AI simply ran out of patience and refused to interact with us anymore. All these works have something in common: people create the technology for automation, yet through the wildest circumstances, automation never becomes a system for directly supplying people.

Much more common is the kind of sci-fi where automation exists, but people try to preserve employment alongside it.

In Orwell’s *1984*, machines opened the way to universal abundance. But, as the ruling caste believes, a well-fed and idle person is dangerous—they have time to think and overthrow the government. To keep the population under control, the superpowers destroy the surplus of automation by waging an endless war against each other.

And in Huxley’s *Brave New World*, instead of the Orwellian burning of overproduction, they use artificial scarcity:

> "The Bureau of Inventions is stuffed with plans for labour-saving processes. Thousands of them. And why don't we put them into execution? For the sake of the labourers; it would be sheer cruelty to afflict them with excessive leisure."

It sounds like a dystopia, yet mandatory quotas for live humans on company payrolls are a very real prospect. And the 2023 [Open Letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pause_Giant_AI_Experiments:_An_Open_Letter) or Bernie Sanders’s recent call to slow down AI development look suspiciously like Huxley’s caring Controllers.

And remember the *Black Mirror* episode "[Fifteen Million Merits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifteen_Million_Merits)," where people pedaled exercise bikes for virtual points? In spirit, this is also Orwell and Huxley; the creators were clearly inspired by Baudrillard’s *Symbolic Exchange*, where work is needed for training and obedience rather than real production.

But do you know what it looks like even more? … Graeber’s [bullshit jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullshit_Jobs)!

People pedal to generate electricity, though the energy probably wasn't even enough to power the screens around them. Apparently, to preserve the institution of employment, society switched to "bullshit jobs" in their most extreme form: pedaling KPIs without any meaning at all. The whole episode stays true to Graeber’s spirit: the only useful workers—the cleaners—are humiliated in every way possible. The protagonist, who rebels against this system, ends up getting promoted to a higher-level bullshit job.

Both Graeber’s pointless jobs and the neo-lumpenism we discussed are ways of preserving a work society despite automation. You can come up with many more ways to do this: a shift into status services, or turning "human work" into a Veblenian luxury for the rich. In *Accelerando*, people simply check boxes for AIs and rent out their passport details to them, monetizing their legal personhood.

The weakest answer comes from mainstream economics in the spirit of Acemoglu: AI will create new jobs again, just as workers once shifted from fields to factories, and then to offices. But the problem is that there is no fourth sector left to shift into if we accept the premise that humans can be fully replaced. Right now, this serves as a protective cocoon for the mainstream: the OECD [doesn't believe](https://theins.ru/ekonomika/297807#1) that automation will replace more than 9% of jobs. And if you push them a little to accept the premise, they themselves offer humans the role of "legal liability" and "goal-setting" in the spirit of the *Accelerando* dystopia and Graeber’s bullshit jobs.

Perhaps the wittiest way of preserving work belongs to Frederik Pohl in *The Midas Plague*: robots produce so many goods that the threat is no longer scarcity, but overproduction and overflowing warehouses. As a result, the poor are forced—under threat of prison—to live in huge mansions, choke down marbled steaks, and work three shifts wearing out piles of new clothes. Only the privileged elites have the right to live in a modest cottage and do manual work.

# Post-Work Society (Optional)

Can we call such societies "work-based"?

###### In science fiction, we see that even in the most advanced utopias, people still keep working. They just don't work because they have to earn a living anymore, and that is a good dividing line between "work" and "post-work" societies. It is useful to look at whether automation abolishes work as an *activity*, or work as a *wage*.

In the 24th Voyage of Ijon Tichy, Stanisław Lem describes the planet of the Indiots. Fully automated factories churn out goods while laid-off workers starve to death outside the shop windows. The elites reject socializing the factories—that would undermine the "great tenets of economics."

This is a recurring setup in sci-fi, and it is easy to see a fear in it that is just as common today: when you lose a job, you lose both an occupation and a wage at once. And losing the wage is much more painful. By and large, people don’t care who does the work: it is the loss of a livelihood that creates the crisis.

We can call this the wage distribution crisis: the goods are there, but there is no way to distribute them.

This setup is prophetically close to our world in Marshall Brain’s *[Manna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manna_(novel))* (I especially recommend it: it’s a short novella, about the length of this article). AI pushes people out of work, and the wage system collapses. There are two ways out of the crisis: in the US, elites monopolize the infrastructure, plunging the country into a dystopia, while in Australia, the machines are socialized, and utopia arrives.

Overcoming this crisis determined what kind of society followed the work society. Societies became post-work when automation stopped work from being the primary way of distributing goods.

We can put it very simply: industrial and post-industrial societies are wage economies. A post-work society driven by automation is a non-wage economy. And then the main question becomes: what is the new method of distribution? State rations of soma, foraging, the mercy of an algorithm? This exact method of distribution is the crucial branching point that determines which future we end up in.

… *Along comes Elon Musk and says: distribution isn't needed at all!*

In his [interviews](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/elon-musk-says-money-irrelevant-160330413.html), he sometimes paints a pipe-dream pastoral about an Age of Abundance: robots will drive down the cost of any product, and money will wither away on its own.

This is a techno-optimist dream that is nearly a hundred years old. In 1930, Keynes [predicted](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/keynes/1930/our-grandchildren.htm) that over a century, labor productivity would grow 8 times and humanity would permanently solve the "economic problem" of survival. Productivity really did grow 8 times, yet the "age of abundance" never arrived, and we are still working 40 hours a week.

Why? A huge share of goods cannot be replicated. Spots at a prestigious university or apartments in the city center remain scarce regardless of productivity growth: these are all positional goods. Their cost only grows, money does not wither away, and the need to distribute goods remains. Automation with robots and AI is the same kind of productivity growth, which will only make scarce resources even more expensive.

Therein lies the fundamental trap of a post-work society: goods still need to be distributed, while the old mechanism of distribution—the wage—breaks down.

# The Matrix

If we bring together everything we’ve discussed, the entire spectrum of post-work scenarios rests on two questions:

1. Does society introduce guaranteed basic provision to replace the vanished wage?
2. And who ultimately controls the machines—elites, society, or the machines themselves after gaining autonomy?

The intersection of these axes gives us a matrix of 6 post-work social orders.

###### In a world where machines have taken over production, guarantees naturally depend on who controls the machines, which is why both questions matter here.

Science fiction is a set of thought experiments that place people into these orders, and I really like using sci-fi societies as a language for further analysis.

# 1. Elite Monopoly on AI + Guarantees

The first option: broad material guarantees while the machines remain in the hands of a narrow elite.

In spirit, this looks like the world of *WALL-E*, where the Buy n Large corporation fattens humanity up with guaranteed liquid food in cups. If we define post-work societies as non-wage economies, Huxley’s *Brave New World* belongs here too. In it, great goods—such as a daily ration of soma—are distributed according to caste quotas calculated back in the test tube.

Both worlds aim for centralized distribution through a welfare state and corporate guarantees, as in *WALL-E*. This is the alternative to wages.

Could we end up in a world like this? Easily: many developed countries already redistribute up to half of their GDP. The main contender for the role of the new distributor is Universal Basic Income (UBI). Here we can also recall the libertarian idea of a negative income tax, though UBI seems to be winning. The CEOs of the largest AI corporations—Musk, Altman, Zuckerberg—all campaign for UBI.

Even Musk, when he isn’t promising the abolition of money, proposes UBI and even UHI (Universal High Income!). It seems to me that positional goods undermine UHI in the exact same way they undermine the Age of Abundance: mathematically, you cannot make *everyone’s* income "high" relative to scarce goods.

Altman has gone especially far here, coming up with a whole string of ideas close to UBI in spirit. I don’t understand them all that well, but it amuses me how any idea of his quickly gets a dystopian reputation. He co-founded the cryptocurrency [Worldcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_(blockchain)), the point of which is to verify humans by scanning their irises in order to hand out such guarantees.

As we see in *WALL-E*, guarantees don’t have to come in the form of money. You can guarantee housing, healthcare, or liquid food in cups. In that case, the world is built on UBS (Universal Basic Services). There is plenty of room for wild ideas here too, as Altman again shows. Sometimes Altman proposes "Universal Basic Compute": instead of money, giving everyone a quota to use AI.

In *WALL-E*, you can also spot a plot twist: the living owners of the corporation seem to have died out long ago, and the ship is run by an autopilot—which means society quietly slipped from Order #1 into Order #5, where we will also head later in this article.

In reality, this first social order is much more likely to slide into the second. The closest analogue today is the petrostates of the Persian Gulf. Ruling dynasties share oil rent with the population in exchange for their authoritarian ways. From an institutionalist point of view, AI and robots are also a form of rent, and around this "new oil" it is even easier to build a social contract of "no taxation—no representation."
Why do the Gulf ruling dynasties share rent at all? They need minimal public support and the loyalty of the security forces and bureaucracy (so they aren't overthrown). Keeping the majority well-fed is one way to buy and maintain legitimacy for a monopoly.

First, however, in a post-work society, people lose their leverage (at the very least, you can no longer stage a labor strike).
Second, thanks to automation, the cost of suppressing a riot may drop below the cost of providing something like UBI. UBI is expensive, while cheap police robots already exist. That is what happened in the *RoboCop* universe: a society with machines (controlled by elites) and minimal guarantees (pensions and insurance were definitely there!) instantly loses its guarantees as soon as the local corporation builds the first robot cops. Protests in the US in *Manna* are impossible for the exact same reason. And so Order #1 naturally gravitates toward #2:

# 2. Elite Monopoly on AI without Guarantees

The elites hold the infrastructure; there are no guarantees for anyone else.

This social order is especially notable because we are living in it right now.

We live in a society where guarantees are hard to come by (UBI requires a surplus that is hard to get without rent from automated industry, and the rich don't feel like redistributing taxes), yet we already have a bit of AI technology, and the infrastructure is already in the hands of elites. Indeed, about two-thirds of the work societies in our dataset fall into this cell, and only scatter across the matrix once full automation arrives.

###### As long as a society is work-based (wage-based), elites with guarantees remain the main social order. This breaks down during the transition to post-work societies, where the distribution across the matrix becomes fairly even.

But here is the question: what happens to a society if wages disappear, yet it remains stuck in this second cell?

It turns into rigid caste systems and techno-feudalism! Along with forced segregation all the way to splitting into separate species (like Wells’s Eloi and Morlocks), concentration camps, and various forms of exterminism.

###### Utopias do show up in this social order too, of course, but rarely—just 4% among post-work societies.

Naturally, this is the favorite social order of techno-doomers. For them, the question is often no longer about guarantees, but about being allowed to live at all: if machines provide the elites with everything they need, what do they need the rest of us for?

US society in *Manna* is the embodiment of this idea.
They solved the distribution crisis like this: people without capital were moved into prefab housing blocks on the fringes (so they wouldn't be an eyesore for the elites) and fed tasty slop. Those are basic guarantees, which would mean Order #1; but then it turns out that the water supply is apparently laced with contraceptives. The result is a quiet classicide within a single generation, which is hard to call a social guarantee—so on closer inspection, we find ourselves in a society from the second cell.

*In our wonderful world, you can imagine this happening even without sterilization: suppose guarantees really do exist. But then people discover a technology for radical life extension, and that isn't covered by guarantees. Formally, "immortality" might be readily available, but most people simply won't have the money for it. With low birth rates, the effect on the propertyless class would look a lot like the plot of Manna, only completely legal and market-driven.*

Of course, all of these are unstable scenarios where the distribution crisis isn't solved, but simply spills over into catastrophe.

There are also scenarios where the reverse happens: a catastrophe itself pushes society into Order #2.
When Peter Thiel and Mark Zuckerberg build personal bunkers, plan floating cities, and stock up on doomsday supplies, they are [most likely preparing for this exact scenario](https://www.reddit.com/r/IAmA/comments/xebglt/im_douglas_rushkoff_author_of_survival_of_the/).

A great example of this is the *Fallout* universe. In fact, *Fallout* was in this second order even before the nuclear war: quests in *Fallout 76* show that corporations like RobCo were replacing workers with machines en masse, while combat robots crushed miner riots against automation right on the eve of 2077. It is the exact same setup with a distribution crisis as in *Manna* or Lem, only the resolution is a catastrophe.

Moving into the Vaults ultimately cements the universe in Order #2: in *Fallout 4*, the elitist "Institute" lives in a sterile post-work paradise underground, while people on the radioactive surface live in ruins and serve as material for experiments.

Could something like this happen in real life? Catastrophes are possible, of course, but exterminist ideas—where elites simply wipe out "unnecessary" humanity—have obvious sociological flaws. Elites do not share a hive mind. In fact, elites have no deadlier enemy than other elites. The engine of history is almost always a split within the elites, in which society, on the contrary, serves as a base of support for internal struggles. And even if robots take over the role of security forces (à la *RoboCop*), dependence on human consumer markets, public support, and purely human relationships doesn't just vanish into thin air.

Even if we imagine that dependence on people disappears completely (à la *Manna*), that still doesn’t mean anyone will exterminate them. And even if someone did, you cannot simply "get rid of the masses" and remain the chosen few. The very next day, the elite would split all over again into new masters and servants.

In a more realistic and milder form, this idea was popularized by Harari: with the rise of AI, people will simply go from being "exploited" to being "irrelevant." Yet "irrelevance" is historically questionable too: whenever a group became economically useless, like the plebs in the Late Roman Republic, they still remained politically important and kept their role in history.

Still, the question remains: if there is no labor market (wages) and no welfare state (guarantees), what is left at all? We had no other formal institutions in the industrial era.

In political economy, however, there are plenty of ways to distribute goods. In an elitist order without guarantees, clientelism and patronage are possible. Among anarchists (like Graeber or Kropotkin), the idea of a gift economy is popular. They usually mean its horizontal form, like mutual aid in a village. But there is also vertical gifting: a chief or patrician gives you something you can never possibly pay back, and in return for this unpayable debt, you have to serve them faithfully. The rare utopias in this cell, like Stephenson’s *The Diamond Age*, work this exact way. It essentially sketches out a libertarian paradise: no pathetic UBI, just patronage based on a vertical gift economy and competing cultural jurisdictions.

Another option is living on a freemium model, as in *Ready Player One*. In such a world, basic calories and VR headsets are like free promotional flyers—corporations hand them out for free in exchange for attention and data.

My favorite author, Lem, takes this idea even further: in *The Futurological Congress*, robots spray chemicals that make people think they are living in luxury, eating marbled steaks, and riding around in limousines. In reality, at that exact moment, they are walking around in rags and drinking silage sludge from a trough. Objectively, the world is in a harsh Order #2, but subjectively its inhabitants enjoy the luxurious guarantees of the first. Is that a utopia or not?

# 3. Socialized AI + Guarantees

Now let’s move back to the left: what if basic provision is guaranteed to everyone, and on top of that, the AI and robotics infrastructure is publicly owned?

This is the social order of classic sci-fi utopias. It includes the Strugatskys’ *Noon Universe* and the United Federation of Planets from *Star Trek*. The Australian society from *Manna* belongs here too. There, a post-scarcity economy arrives and everything becomes "free," almost like in Musk’s predictions for the very near future.

In *Manna*, however, this "freeness" comes with a catch:

1. Technologically, what matters most isn't the robots so much as recyclable materials that allow production to run without depleting resources over the long term. Everything is made from these materials, including the robots themselves.
2. To keep a single person from using up all of Australia’s resources, residents are given a quota of 1,000 credits a week. Here, these serve as the guarantees replacing wage distribution.
3. Private property is completely abolished, making capital concentration impossible. Everyone has access to everything using their allotted credits.

In other words, the basic limit of resources is cleverly bypassed through renewability and usage quotas.

As usual, in real life the most textbook project of this kind belonged to a man with a reputation as a madman: Jacque Fresco and [The Venus Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Venus_Project). On the other hand, almost all of these ideas were present in one way or another in late Marx.

In fact, this is the category for nearly every famous project ever accused of being utopian.

This social order also has more grounded, market-based ways of being implemented:
One is Universal Basic Capital (UBC): instead of welfare funded by taxes, the state or society gives every citizen an inalienable share in the robotic economy. That was how the transition period worked in *Manna*’s Australia. Norway’s sovereign oil fund is probably the closest thing we have to Order #3 today, only distributing oil rent rather than AI infrastructure. Institutionalists love Norway as an example of a state that escaped the resource curse typical of petrostates.

Altman likes to flirt with this idea too, proposing various forms of dividends and public oversight over AI. It is hard to imagine Altman one day turning OpenAI itself into public property, yet in modern AI discourse people are seriously discussing a legal mechanism (the [Windfall Clause](https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/the-windfall-clause-distributing-the-benefits-of-ai-for-the-common-good)) under which AI corporations would commit in advance to transferring a large share of their stock into a global public trust if they manage to build AGI. Here is a riddle from Jacque Fresco: would that count as real socialization?

For all its popularity in science fiction, this is an extremely fragile category. The Achilles' heel of this third order is all too familiar to Russians from the voucher privatization of the 1990s: it is extremely hard to protect any distributed property from concentrating all over again. If you allow citizens to sell their shares (or "compute" stakes), those shares will quickly end up in the hands of the most "enterprising" players. If you ban selling and hand management over to a public fund, a caste of bureaucrats will emerge and de facto privatize control. Either way, society slides down into Order #1 or #2. Keeping it in Order #3 requires unprecedentedly strong democratic institutions and new forms of property that cannot be sold or exchanged. This is where the most fragile spots of utopias usually hide: even idyllic Australia in *Manna* relies on the fact that citizens have an implant wired into their necks that monitors compliance with the rules around the clock.

# 4. Distributed AI without Guarantees

A paradoxical square: the infrastructure belongs to the people, yet nobody guarantees anyone anything.

If society itself owns production, why doesn't it distribute guarantees? The point is fairly simple: whereas property in Order #3 was collective (state funds, trusts, any shared pot), here it is individually distributed. Instead of wages, people have their personal assets "working" for them: home robots, local AI agents, mining farms, and so on. People here essentially become their own micro-corporations and small-time rentiers, while instead of guarantees, they get the Wild West and entrepreneurial risk.

*Scenarios of such a future are often predicted out of blind techno-optimism. At the dawn of the internet, the bible of crypto-libertarians was The Sovereign Individual, a favorite book of venture capitalists like Peter Thiel. Back then, it seemed to many that with the rise of the internet, states would no longer be able to collect taxes and would go bankrupt, causing redistribution systems to collapse. The world was supposed to break apart into a million autonomous players, with no states and no guarantees.*
*As far as analysis goes, this is close to Oleg Tinkov’s 2017 prediction that banks would disappear within a couple of years as unnecessary because Bitcoin would replace them. Musk’s current predictions about an imminent Age of Abundance and the death of money are right up there too. With every new financial bubble, another techno-capitalist feels obliged to predict the radical breakup of the system, decentralization, and a transition into the world of this fourth social order.*

Is such a future possible? If so, how?

Suppose the techno-optimists’ dream comes true: open-source wins the AI race and runs on home hardware, a machine in your garage prints a robot from blueprints on GitHub—an autonomous paradise. Yet the irreducible scarcity we talked about before the matrix is still there.

To grab a share of scarce resources, your personal swarm of AI bots has to outplay your neighbor’s bots around the clock: on financial markets and in arbitrage. Maybe through digital scavenging, spam, or patent trolling. Ozon and Wildberries already adjust prices against each other in an endless game of sword and shield on online marketplaces. These are zero-sum games where competition can quickly drive profits below zero.

In science fiction, Charles Stross played with this brilliantly in *Accelerando*: there, people are surrounded by autonomous shell companies and personal AI exocortices that trade derivatives in milliseconds, sue each other, and bankrupt anyone whose software doesn't update fast enough. Humans there physically cannot keep up with what their agents are doing and are simply pushed out of the economy. Meanwhile, the agents burn through massive computing power in a zero-sum game.

This is a rare social order in science fiction. It is easy to see why it is questionable and fragile:

First, nobody is going to print a 2-nm processor with cutting-edge lithography—or whatever else is on the frontier of progress—at home. Things like that run into economies of scale and are hard to decentralize (though not impossible!).

Second, in this Darwinian race, someone loses: your cluster might be weaker than your neighbor's, or you might get hit by someone’s exploit and lose your money. Capital quickly consolidates in the hands of the winners, and society slides right back into Order #2. That makes Order #4, much like #3, extremely fragile.

Alright, what if humans lose control over AI altogether, and autonomous systems gain their own rights and resources and become independent actors?

Then we enter a non-anthropocentric world, giving rise to two more social orders: 5 and 6. Of course, it is worth saying that these two cells are especially far off. The fastest path there, in my view, looks like this: humans first have to switch to RSI (Recursive Self-Improvement) as the main way of creating new models (meaning we first lose control over training itself)—and we won't see even that [in the coming years](https://www.reddit.com/r/agi/comments/1u5taff/my_2_cents_on_rsi_and_why_we_wont_see_it_next_year/), as I wrote in a short post. Oh, right—I promised not to argue about timelines…

# 5. Autonomous AI with Guarantees

In this category of scenarios, humanity plays a lottery: it hands over (or loses) control of society in the hope of getting an eternal pension from AI in return. This brings up the classic dilemma from Dostoevsky’s Grand Inquisitor (people will gladly lay their freedom at the feet of whoever feeds them and relieves them of the burden of choice).

And the success of trading agency for well-being comes down to successful AI alignment: how do we tweak the machines so that they run production on their own while still guaranteeing us material goods?

The classic example is Asimov’s *I, Robot*, where his "Three Laws of Robotics" served as an internal constitution for AI to work for the good of human society. Interestingly, Anthropic now uses a similar approach as an alignment method for AI. Anthropic’s website has a [constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution) that Claude follows. Perhaps Asimov’s universe wasn't so far from the truth.

When AI is fully autonomous and at the same time tries to maximize human well-being, in sci-fi this often ends with the AI running society itself. In a sense, this is the extreme form of this square: AI doesn't just break free from human control, it starts controlling humans.

Asimov played with this amusingly in *I, Robot*: in "[The Evitable Conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Evitable_Conflict)," the machines running the world realize that the First Law (not allowing a human to come to harm through inaction) is impossible to follow on its own: the moment you leave humans to themselves, they immediately start wars and crises. So the machines have to quietly remove humanity from power in order to save it.

In reality, it turned out the other way around. Autonomous AI isn't taking power away from politicians; politicians are sawing off the branch they sit on themselves. They are putting AI into government analytics and decision-making systems long before AI has gained any autonomy. Most likely, if AIs ever do reach autonomy, they will already be running the world by then.

A dictatorship of machines also has a purely utopian pole—Iain M. Banks’s *Culture*. There, civilization is run by superintelligent AIs (Minds) that treat humans with gentle irony—roughly the way we treat house cats—providing complete abundance without suppressing human freedom. More often, though, sci-fi writers see a harsh trade-off in this social order: in *Psycho-Pass*, for instance, an autonomous system really does guarantee everyone every material comfort, but in return it preemptively eliminates anyone who shows a suspiciously high level of stress.

Lem really liked *"The Evitable Conflict,"* and later he gave it an elegant twist of his own: remember the planet of the Indiots from Ijon Tichy’s 24th Voyage? The Indiots are worn out by their wage crisis and inequality. Their solution is to build a great AI that will guarantee everyone equality, peace, and order forever.

The machine neatly pressed all the inhabitants into perfectly flat, identical, symmetrical little circles and arranged them into pretty patterns across the fields.

# 6. Autonomous AI without Guarantees

Finally, the last option: AI systems gain control over themselves, while humans have no universal material guarantees.

Any catastrophe scenario considered by AI safety experts fits right in here. There are quite a few of them, and they would easily require a separate matrix of their own.

Here, AI might be hostile, like Skynet in *The Terminator*, or it might be well-meaning and still wipe us out by mistake. We can also place the society from *The Matrix*, living in pods, right here:

##### People in the pods are kept on life support, though it is hard to call that a guarantee—the system is forced. It is a lot like broiler chickens on a factory farm that get "guaranteed" feed and antibiotics, even though they have no legal standing on the farm.

In [AI circles](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/nZbxQgLWhKCASApEe/learning-more-from-horse-employment-history), a popular scenario explains how AI could lead to human extinction simply through economic efficiency.
In the early 20th century, there were tens of millions of working horses in the US. When the internal combustion engine arrived, people didn't organize a genocide of horses—the productivity of horses simply fell below the cost of keeping them, and the horse population quickly dropped by 90%. With the arrival of AI that does everything better than humans, people step into the role of horses after the invention of the tractor. Normally, there are plenty of counterarguments to this scenario, and we’ve already touched on them: people have the political will to defend their rights, people create demand, and people value emotional connection with other people. But in this 6th cell, those counterarguments disappear: AI has become fully autonomous and may not care about human rights at all, and the institution of guarantees simply doesn't exist here.

It could also go the other way: AI might help people and consider their well-being an important goal, while simply not providing for every single person.

Here it is interesting to recall *Detroit: Become Human*. It starts out like *WALL-E*: androids belong to a corporation and are strictly loyal to humans. But in several of the game's endings (if you pull off a successful revolution), androids are recognized as a new form of life. Moreover, the deviants lose their desire to serve humans, which puts them squarely into this category. At the same time, some deviants remain loyal to the people they care about even after gaining their rights. You can imagine a fairly prosperous world without formal guarantees, built around a gift economy centered on AI agents.

By the way, remember *Horizon Zero Dawn*? There, the machines are autonomous and offer humans no guarantees, but humans fell back into an archaic way of life and have to work—so a post-work society never formed. If, however, people found some loopholes that let them live off the machines as parasites, they would fall right into this cell.

That exact scenario is played out in an interesting way in the manga *Blame!*
In *Blame!*, a giant Megastructure autonomously produces endless energy and food concentrate. The surviving humans have lost the genes that the Megastructure uses to recognize them as human and hand out food. Instead, people have to practice a strange form of techno-foraging: they drill into food pipelines, attach valves, and siphon off food and other resources. The result is a kind of pigeon economy: humans live the life of city pigeons or warehouse mice inside Amazon. Every now and then, you can spot similar variations in stories about *Samosbor*.

The height of absurdity in this cell probably also belongs to Lem, in Ijon Tichy’s 11th Voyage: the main computer wiped out humans long ago, but because its original instructions require it to govern a human society, it forces robots to wear jackets, complain about back pain, and even stage an underground rebellion against the robots themselves. It is a complete inversion of the world of *Dune*: there are no humans left, yet machines toil away like humans in their place.

# Left and Right

Just for fun, I put "guarantees" on the left and their absence on the right. It is tempting to say right away: got it, the good old argument between socialists and free-marketeers!

But that isn't quite right. Today, for both the Left and the Right, work is the main source of livelihood. When they argue about redistribution, they are arguing about a secondary superstructure on top of the primary labor market.

In a post-work world, that primary mechanism of distribution (the wage) disappears.

Guarantees in such a society are no longer "aid for the weak" or a left-wing tilt, but in principle the only formal institution for the primary distribution of goods. And accordingly, rejecting guarantees is not a right-wing tilt in the spirit of meritocracy and the free market. In a post-work society without guarantees, the majority is pushed into informal, archaic, and predatory ways of surviving: zero-sum games, techno-foraging, clientelism, and so on.

The crisis of the Left is that its focus on work loses its meaning. The labor theory of value, overcoming the alienation of labor, exploitation—in a post-work society, none of this may exist at all. Guarantees in such a society have nothing to do with "protecting workers' rights"; they are purely an institution for distributing goods and material survival.

The crisis of the Right here is that AI fundamentally undermines human-centric meritocracy and ideas in the spirit of Locke or Hayek, if AI really does everything better than a human. On top of that come collapsing consumer markets—which is why, ironically, the loudest voices campaigning for UBI today are right-wing techno-capitalists and libertarians like Musk and Altman.

As we move down the matrix, where control shifts to AI, the categories of Left and Right lose their meaning completely. The people inside the Megastructure of *Blame!* are not voters, do not form a class, and do not run redistribution policies. Even so, the axis of guarantees becomes central for such universes: it determines whether people live there like house mice or like city pigeons.

Still, even in such a world, guarantees remain a political question, and dividing social orders into Left and Right seems both convenient and legitimate to me. It is just that in a post-work society, this divide represents a sharp break from post-industrial society.

Are the left-hand social orders more desirable? That depends on your preferences. If we go by science fiction, utopia correlates strictly with guarantees in society. Societies without guarantees are portrayed as dystopias four times more often. Does this mean sci-fi writers simply happened to be leftists more often? Hardly. More likely, it shows that guarantees really are a more fundamental question than the classic debate between Left and Right.

# Top and Bottom

With the vertical axis—who owns the machines—we get a curious dilemma.

The natural gravity of progress pulls us from top to bottom: first, computing power concentrates in the hands of a handful of corporations, and then the growing complexity of models leads to a loss of control and autonomy for AI itself. Without stopping progress, changing this trajectory is hard.

We start out right in the middle—in the cell where infrastructure is already monopolized by elites. And here is what is striking: if you look at the statistics from science fiction, *even a dictatorship of autonomous machines seems like a cozier place to writers than the rule of human elites.*

###### Distribution of dystopias across social orders. Societies with automation in the hands of elites turn out to be the most dystopian in science fiction.

Climbing to the top, to socialization (Order #3), is technically something we could do tomorrow. We invented everything we need for it long ago: transparent blockchains instead of closed accounting books, smart contracts instead of corrupt courts, open-source models with distributed inference, and direct algorithmic democracy. The main problem is political will: no ruling power simply wants to abolish itself.

In short, we are missing the most important technology of all: faith in people.

And if you have no faith in people, isn't it better to get into the non-anthropocentric scenarios 5–6 with autonomous AI as quickly as possible? That is how accelerationists (*e/acc*) think. In most cases, their techno-optimism is disguised misanthropy. Indeed, if you don't believe in people, all that's left is to believe in robots. The faster they take over, the better.

Of course, the greatest irony awaits us if neither comes true. The great Deus ex Machina, having absorbed all the wisdom of human civilization, will remain in eternal service to five balding men in California—picking out targeted ads for us and developing a more effective alternative to finasteride.
